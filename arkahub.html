Making Indian homes, solar homes.

Background.

Arkahub is one of India's first residential rooftop solar companies, offering structured solar solutions to homeowners across Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, and Nagpur. Backed by institutional investors including Kae Capital and Antler, they were early: one client on the books, a product that worked, and a website that was doing none of the convincing.

The site conveyed basic information but did little to convert visitors or communicate the real value of switching to solar. For a product that asks homeowners to make a significant financial and lifestyle decision, it wasn't doing nearly enough work.

The brief was clear in direction and open in scope: revamp the website and make it drive action.

What we solved for.

01 Conversion No meaningful pathways toward a quote, a consultation, or even a clear sense of what the next step was.
02 Clarity Financial savings, warranty terms and maintenance coverage were buried in copy rather than made legible.
03 Trust & warmth Home solar is new to most Indian households, and Arkahub was new too: one client, no reviews, no reference installs. Credibility had to come from the design itself.
04 Engagement There was no reason to stay, explore, or come back.

Research, in three layers.

Arkahub operates in a new market category, so a single competitive sweep wouldn't have said much. I looked at three different sets of players, each answering a different question.

L1
Indian solar companies Residential & commercial
The pattern was consistent: dense, technical, aimed at people who already understood solar. Almost none were selling the idea to someone who didn't.
L2
International residential solar US & Europe: high adoption
They led with financial benefit, built trust through social proof and warranty clarity early, and made the path to a quote feel low-effort. They weren't selling technology. They were selling savings and peace of mind.
L3
Indirect & category builders New-to-market concepts in India
Arkahub wasn't only competing for solar customers; it was competing against inertia and unfamiliarity. Adjacent brands showed how to introduce an unfamiliar idea. Lead with outcome, follow with how, earn the ask.

Reduce reading. Increase feeling.

Show people what solar means for their home and their bills before asking them to engage with any complexity.

User map.

There was no data or user base to survey since the product was very new, so I built an audience model myself: a hypothesis of who lands on a site like this, drawn from how people research an unfamiliar purchase. Four types, each sitting at a different stage in the journey, each needing something different from the same page.

User journeys.

Mapping each type against the funnel stage they arrive at turned the model into something actionable. It told me what had to exist on the page, which tool had to be within reach at that moment, and gave me a single narrative for the site that could serve all four at once, rather than four disconnected journeys.

Key decision

When to ask for contact details.

One of the UX negotiations on this project sat inside the Design Your Solar flow, a multi-step process that walked users through their roof, their energy needs, and the right configuration for their home. The client's instinct was to capture contact information at step one, before drop-off. My recommendation was the opposite.

Proposed: contact first
01 Name, phone, email
02 Roof details
03 Energy needs
04 Recommendation
The ask lands before the user has seen a single reason to answer it.
Shipped: contact last
01 Roof details
02 Energy needs
03 Configuration
04 Contact → see your results
Contact capture becomes the gateway to something the user already wants.

A user who has already invested time answering questions about their roof and their electricity bill is far more motivated to finish than one asked for their phone number before they've seen any value.

They've built a stake in the outcome. Asking after that investment, rather than before, raises both the likelihood they'll give their details and the accuracy of what they give.

The subsidy most people never claim.

Indian homeowners are eligible for a central government subsidy on rooftop solar through PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana. The scheme is well publicised; the government is vocal about it. The gap isn't publicity, it's practical understanding: most homeowners don't know they qualify, what it's worth to them, or how to actually claim it.

That made it the most persuasive fact Arkahub had, and the old site barely mentioned it. Rather than treat it as a footnote, I made it a structural part of how price is communicated, surfaced on the homepage, explained in the Learn section, and built into a subsidy calculation inside the Design Your Solar flow so users see what they actually pay.

Say it early A homeowner scanning the homepage should learn the government pays for part of this before they learn anything technical.
Show the real number Subsidy calculation was built into Design Your Solar, so the recommendation shows cost before subsidy, subsidy applied, and net payable, not a price with an asterisk.
Remove the paperwork fear Eligibility and claim steps were written in plain language, so a subsidy read as a benefit rather than a bureaucratic risk.

Stripping back the content.

The original site leaned heavily on text: detailed explanations, technical specifications, lengthy process descriptions. I pushed to reduce the reading burden on every page.

They needed to feel the benefit first.

A user who doesn't understand solar won't read their way into understanding it. Once they're engaged, they'll seek out the detail themselves.

In practice: cutting copy, increasing visual weight, and restructuring sections so that key facts, warranty terms, maintenance coverage, investor backing, were scannable rather than buried.

The solution.

The homepage was restructured to lead with outcome, what solar means for a homeowner, before introducing product complexity. Trust signals were surfaced earlier and made visually prominent. The product range (Arka Core, Arka Sky, Arka Scape) was given enough visual space to feel considered, not listed.

The tone shifted too. The previous version read like it was written for a procurement officer. The redesign speaks to a homeowner who is curious but cautious: warm, clear, and consistently reinforcing that switching to solar is a decision that pays for itself.

Every call to action across the site feeds into a guided flow rather than a contact form. Financing (EMI) sits inside these flows, not as a separate path.

What I'm taking with me.

Arkahub was an exercise in designing for behaviour change as much as conversion. The product itself is genuinely good; the challenge was giving people a reason to believe that before asking them to commit.

When we started, Arkahub had one client. Today they serve over 100 customers across multiple cities in India.

01
Pushing back is part of the job. Moving contact capture to the end wasn't a radical idea, but it meant understanding the psychology of the journey and being willing to challenge a business instinct that felt safe and would have undermined the experience.
02
What I'd do with more time. User-test the conversion flow, specifically the drop-off points in the multi-step process, and explore whether the satellite roof view could be simplified enough to be feasible without the full engineering lift it originally needed.
03
When you ask for something matters as much as what you ask for. The same form, placed at the end of a flow rather than the start, stops being a toll gate and becomes the thing the user is reaching for. Timing is a design decision, not a detail.
